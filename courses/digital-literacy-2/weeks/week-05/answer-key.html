Instructor guide. Accept equivalent evidence-based answers. These activities evaluate demonstrated skills, not speed.

1. Adjust one thing at your station so you can read without leaning in: chair height, screen distance (about an arm’s length) or text size. Or try Magnifier: press Windows logo key and +, then Windows logo key and Esc to close it. Write what you changed and what got easier, then put it back.

A specific change (chair, screen about an arm’s length away with the top at or slightly below eye level, text size or Magnifier) and a benefit the learner can see; no medical claims. Settings restored.

2. Play the first 10 seconds of the slide 14 video with the sound muted and read the captions. Then press Tab a few times and watch the outline move from control to control. Name two design features that help more people, and who each one helps.

Universal design makes tools usable by more people without special changes. Examples: captions (noisy room, hearing loss), keyboard access (cannot use a mouse), clear text labels that do not rely on color.

3. On slide 8, select the subject, the sender and the link to inspect each one. Write two warning signs, the independent check you would use instead (a real website or a number you already trust), and what you would do if you had already typed your password.

Signs: pressure to act now, a sender or link that does not match the organization (account-check.example), a request for a password. Check: the real website from a bookmark or typed address, or a number from a card or letter, never the message’s link or number. If a password was typed: change it on the real site, turn on two-step verification and tell the instructor.

4. Name the risk and one safe next step for each: (a) a new online friend asks you to keep a money request secret; (b) you stay up late scrolling because you do not want to miss anything; (c) someone posts something hurtful about you in a public group.

(a) Possible catfishing: send no money and keep no secrets; verify another way. (b) Fear of missing out: set a stopping point or quiet alerts with Do not disturb. (c) Do not reply in anger; keep screenshots if safe; report or block; talk to someone you trust.

5. Say your choice for each card on slide 9 before you flip it. Then look without changing anything: in Edge, open Settings from the ··· menu, search permissions and open Camera; in Windows 10, open Start › Settings › Update & Security › Windows Update (Windows 11: Start › Settings › Windows Update). Write one thing each page shows.

USB: do not connect; give it to staff. Camera: deny when the task does not need it. Benefits fee: do not pay or use the link; VA never charges a fee to apply; call a number from VA.gov. Windows 10: free security updates ended October 2025; use Extended Security Updates or a supported device. One real detail from each page; nothing changed or installed.

6. In Word, open a blank document and select File › Info › Protect Document. Write which option asks for a password to open and encrypts the file, and which only limits changes. Do not set a password. Then write what device encryption protects and what you do before you step away from a signed-in computer.

Encrypt with Password sets a password to open and encrypts the file; Word cannot recover a lost password. Always Open Read-Only and Restrict Editing only limit changes; the text is still readable. Device encryption protects a lost or stolen computer that is turned off; lock the screen with Windows logo key and L before stepping away.

7. Skills challenge, on your own: refine a search, build a structured paragraph, total four costs with a formula, bring back deleted text and write useful feedback. Leave your work on screen for your instructor.

Rate each row Independent, With prompt or Needs practice on the paper roster, using the evidence list below. The quick card counts as Independent; any spoken prompt is With prompt. Keep this separate from the post-test score.

Evidence to look for on each row:

  1. Refine a search. Adds a place (Beckley WV) and a time (the current year) to the search box, and says why the narrower search gives a more useful list.
  2. Build a structured paragraph. The title uses Heading 1, the steps are a Word numbered list (select one number and all three highlight), and the warning line is bold.
  3. Total four costs. B6 shows 29 and the formula bar shows =SUM(B2:B5) or =B2+B3+B4+B5, not a typed number. Check before the file is closed: a .csv file keeps the result, not the formula.
  4. Bring back deleted text. Presses Ctrl and Z (or Undo) while the file is still open and the line returns; says Undo is lost once the file closes, and that version history works for files saved in OneDrive.
  5. Write useful feedback. Names at least two missing details (a.m. or p.m., the date, the room), why a reader needs them, and one specific, polite fix.

8. Take the post-test on your own. Print page 1 of your results or save it as a PDF, and write your score out of 28. Name one strength and one skill to practice next.

Page 1 printed or saved; score out of 28 on the roster; a named strength and a specific next task tied to a results domain.
