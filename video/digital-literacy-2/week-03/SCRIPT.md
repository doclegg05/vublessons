## Start with the reader
A useful handout begins with a reader and a task. Imagine a neighbor who wants computer help. Put the service and next action near the beginning. Leave out details that do not help the person decide what to do, and explain unfamiliar terms.

## Give the document structure
Use real heading styles for sections, a short numbered list for steps, and link text that explains the destination. Making a line bold can change its appearance without creating a heading. Check that the structure still makes sense when you read the document in order.

## Check a small calculation
In a practice workbook, enter paper at twelve dollars, folders at eight, and pens at five. The total is twenty-five. Use a sum formula over all three cells. Change paper to fifteen and check that the total becomes twenty-eight. This tests the formula rather than its appearance.

## Choose the output for the job
The right file format depends on the next person's task. An editable document supports revision. A PDF often preserves a finished layout. A CSV carries plain table data but loses formatting and formulas. Choose deliberately, then reopen the exported file and check what survived.

## Show your evidence
Create a small resource pack: a structured handout, a checked workbook, and three clear slides. Credit any reused material according to its license. Show a partner the calculation and the exported handout. Ask them to find the next action without your help, then improve anything unclear.
