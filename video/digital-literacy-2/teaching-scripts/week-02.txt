Find information for a real decision|search|0
Imagine a neighbor asks where they can get help using a computer. You may already have a familiar library, community center, or veterans' organization in mind. Use that experience as a starting point, then verify what is available now. Today's search tools answer in full sentences, so our first skill is asking well, and our second is checking what the answer says. Our task is to find a suitable public service, record enough evidence to revisit it, and organize a fictional handout without exposing private information. You can use a different local service that matters to you. The names and details in this demonstration are examples, not a claim about any particular organization. A useful search ends with a supported decision, not just an answer that sounds confident.

Ask the way you would ask a person|search|0
Search tools now answer in sentences. Google may show an AI Overview above the results, and AI Mode lets you keep asking follow-up questions. Both work best when you ask the way you would ask a librarian. Say what you need, where you are, and what matters to you. Where can I get free computer help near Beckley, West Virginia, in the evening? is far more useful than computer help. If the first answer is too broad, ask a follow-up, such as which of these are open on Saturdays. Pause and write a question for your chosen community task. Include the service, the place, and one detail that matters to you. Then ask one follow-up.

Read the answer, then open the source|verify|4
An AI answer is a quick summary written from many pages. It can sound sure and still be wrong, out of date, or about a different town. So treat it as a lead, not a source. Look for the links beside or beneath it, and open the organization's own page. Ask who published it, when it was updated, and whether it answers your question. If the summary and the page disagree, trust the responsible organization, and confirm hours by phone before you travel. Never type private details into a question, such as a Social Security number, a bank number, or medical information. Pause, open one source from your answer, and find one detail it confirms and one it does not.

Leave a source trail for your future self|document|0
Suppose you find a useful service today and need it again next month. A note saying good website will not be enough. Record the organization, the page title, the web address, the date you checked it, and the particular fact you plan to use. If an AI answer led you to the page, record the page it pointed to, not the summary. A bookmark helps you return, but it does not freeze the page or prove that the information remains current. In your handout, distinguish a checked fact from your own interpretation. If the page does not answer a question, write needs confirmation rather than filling the gap with a guess. This is the digital equivalent of writing the source beside a number in a household notebook.

Fill a form with the minimum useful information|form|0
An online form is a structured request. Read its purpose and required fields before typing. In our course practice form, choose the kind of computer help needed and a fictional contact preference. Review the entries before selecting the final button. On a real service, ask whether a sensitive detail is necessary for the task, and verify the site before entering it. Our practice does not send a real request. That distinction matters: seeing a confirmation message in a simulator proves that you completed the exercise, not that an organization received anything. Pause, complete the fictional form, and explain what you deliberately left out and why.

Give your files a home you can recognize|sync|0
Think of a folder as a labeled place to put related work. Create a practice folder for the community resource task and use a descriptive file name, such as library-help-draft. A file saved on this computer may not appear on another computer. A cloud file belongs to a service and an account, and a synced folder may keep copies in more than one place. Before moving to another device, check the account and the saving or syncing status. Reopen the file from the intended location. That simple test catches a common mistake: editing one copy while later opening an older copy somewhere else.

Choose access by what the partner must do|permission|1
Our partner should suggest clearer wording without rewriting the handout directly. Viewer access usually allows reading. Commenter access, when supported, allows suggestions through comments. Editor access permits changes to the content. Choose the smallest role that fits the task, inspect the recipient carefully, and have the partner verify that the file opens with the expected controls. A link that works for you may still fail for someone else. Also distinguish read-only from encryption. Read-only limits editing; it does not necessarily prevent someone from reading or copying the information. Pause and choose a role for someone who only needs to read the finished handout. Viewer is the appropriate starting point.

Separate sync from a recovery copy|sync|2
Sync is convenient because a change can travel between a device and an online service. The same convenience can carry a deletion. Watch the practice file disappear from one location and then the other. That is why sync alone is not a separate backup. A backup provides another recovery copy under an appropriate plan. Version history may preserve earlier content, while a recycle bin may retain a deleted file for a limited time. Availability and retention depend on the service. Do not test deletion with your only important file. Use the fictional copy and explain which protection would help if the mistake spread through every synced location.

Recover the right thing and package carefully|recovery|3
First identify the problem. If the whole file is missing after deletion, inspect the appropriate trash or recycle bin. If the file exists but its wording changed, inspect version history. Preview the earlier version and coordinate with collaborators before restoring it. You do not want to replace useful work someone just added. Compression is a separate tool: a ZIP file can package several files for transfer and may reduce their size. It is not automatically encryption or a backup plan. After extracting a practice ZIP, open a file to confirm the contents are usable. Choose the tool that matches the problem rather than trusting a familiar icon.

Show the evidence behind your choices|verify|4
Pause and build a small resource folder for your chosen community task. Include a source note, a fictional handout, and a clear file name. Tell a partner which fact you verified, why that source is appropriate, and which permission fits the review task. Then explain how you would recover a deleted file and how that differs from restoring earlier wording. If an account or sharing feature is unavailable, demonstrate the decision with the course simulator and label it simulated. For your next independent task, reuse this sequence: ask a focused question, check the evidence, record a trail, organize the file, and verify access.
