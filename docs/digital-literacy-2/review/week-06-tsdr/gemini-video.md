## Evidence by dimension

### 1. Accuracy and correctness
*   **1:15**: The narrator states, "A convincing sign-in screen does not prove secure authentication exists behind it," which is a crucial and accurate security warning for novices building prototypes.
*   **3:28**: The instruction to "not put passwords or private API keys into the prompt" is an essential, correct safety practice for using AI tools.
*   **4:14**: The warning to "Be careful that the editor has not silently added a text file extension" (resulting in `.html.txt`) is a highly accurate and common pitfall for Windows users using basic text editors.

### 2. Clarity of explanation
*   **0:45**: The visual distinction between a Website (read), Web app (input/result), and SaaS (ongoing service) provides a very clear mental model for beginners.
*   **1:31**: The video uses a relatable analogy of a "familiar form" to explain the abstract concepts of HTML (structure), CSS (appearance), and JavaScript (rules/behavior).
*   **2:13**: The concept of "acceptance checks" is clearly explained using a simple, three-row table showing the input, expected result, and actual observation.

### 3. Pacing and cognitive load for older adults
*   **0:00–7:29**: The narration is nearly continuous for seven and a half minutes. For older adults with low tech confidence, this density of new concepts (HTML, CSS, JS, AI prompting, regression testing) will cause cognitive overload if not paused.
*   **3:00**: The "bounded request" quadrant graphic contains a lot of text to read while the narrator is simultaneously speaking about the concepts, splitting the learner's attention.
*   **5:54**: The explanation of regression testing ("retest what worked") is introduced very quickly alongside the concept of fixing a broken feature, which may be difficult to grasp in one pass.

### 4. Practice prompts
*   **2:02**: The narrator asks learners to "Open the supplied starter in the browser and identify a visible example of each part," but the narration continues immediately to the next sentence without a pause.
*   **2:48**: The narrator says "Pause and write three checks..." but only 6 seconds elapse before the next instruction.
*   **6:34**: The narrator says "Pause and make one small improvement or repair," but only 4 seconds elapse before the narration resumes.

### 5. Audio–visual alignment
*   **1:31**: As the narrator names HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, the corresponding icon on screen highlights in yellow, perfectly syncing visual focus with the audio.
*   **4:27**: When the narrator says "Type library into the search field," the word "library" appears in the simulated search box exactly on cue.
*   **5:11**: When discussing moving through controls with the keyboard, a visual "Tab ->" indicator appears on screen precisely as the narrator mentions it.

### 6. Screen demonstrations
*   **3:44**: The mouse cursor is a standard, small arrow without a highlight circle, making it difficult for users with reduced vision to track its movement across the screen.
*   **4:27**: When text is entered into the search field ("library", "zzz"), it appears instantaneously rather than being typed out character-by-character, which can be jarring and hard to follow for novices.
*   **4:52**: The action of clicking the dropdown and selecting "Learning" happens very quickly, without lingering on the open menu to show the options.

### 7. Visual design and legibility
*   **0:45**: The use of large, simple icons and high-contrast yellow/white text on a dark blue background is excellent for older eyes and projector viewing.
*   **3:44**: The HTML code shown in the "Practice Text Editor" is too small and dense. At 720p on a classroom projector, learners with reduced vision will struggle to read the tags.
*   **5:11**: The simulated browser interface is clean, uncluttered, and uses large input fields, which is a good model for accessible design.

### 8. Narration quality
*   **0:00–7:29**: The pronunciation is perfectly clear and the audio is free of artifacts, pops, or background noise.
*   **0:21**: The delivery of encouraging lines like "You do not need to become a professional programmer today" sounds somewhat robotic and lacks the natural warmth and inflection a human instructor would use to build confidence.
*   **4:26**: The pacing of the speech is very uniform; there are no natural conversational pauses between sentences to let ideas settle.

### 9. Relevance and motivation
*   **0:05**: Using the task of finding a "community resource" (like a library or makers group) grounds the technical exercise in a real-world task that is highly relevant to veterans.
*   **0:21**: Explicitly stating that the goal is not to become a "professional programmer" lowers the barrier to entry and reduces anxiety for learners with low tech confidence.
*   **6:54**: Explaining that a local prototype is a "successful learning result" validates the learners' work, even if it isn't a fully hosted public app.

### 10. Accessibility
*   **1:31**: The narrator fully describes the function of HTML, CSS, and JS, so a learner who cannot see the screen can still understand the concepts.
*   **4:27**: When testing the search, the narrator says "compare the result" but does not explicitly read the on-screen text ("1 fictional resource found"), meaning a visually impaired learner would miss the specific outcome of the test.
*   **5:11**: The video explicitly teaches keyboard accessibility (using the Tab key) and screen resizing, reinforcing good accessibility practices for the learners themselves.

## Top issues, ranked

1.  **Severity: High** | **Timestamps: 2:02, 2:48, 6:34**
    *   **Problem:** The video asks learners to pause and do hands-on tasks but provides zero actual silence or visual hold time.
    *   **Why it matters:** Older adults and novices need time to transition from listening to doing. If the video keeps playing, they will fall behind, panic, and miss the subsequent instructions.
    *   **Recommended fix:** Instructor note (no edit). The instructor must manually pause the video at these timestamps.
2.  **Severity: Medium** | **Timestamp: 3:44**
    *   **Problem:** The HTML code displayed in the text editor simulation is too small to read comfortably on a 720p classroom projector.
    *   **Why it matters:** Learners with reduced vision will not be able to see the structure of the code being discussed, causing frustration and disengagement.
    *   **Recommended fix:** Re-edit visuals to zoom in on the specific lines of code being discussed, or increase the font size in the simulation.
3.  **Severity: Medium** | **Timestamps: 4:27, 4:32, 4:42**
    *   **Problem:** Text entry in the screen demonstrations happens instantaneously rather than being animated character-by-character.
    *   **Why it matters:** Novices rely on seeing the process of interaction. Instantaneous changes look like magic and make it hard to follow what the user actually did.
    *   **Recommended fix:** Re-edit visuals to simulate natural typing speed and add a subtle highlight circle around the mouse cursor.
4.  **Severity: Low** | **Timestamps: 0:00–7:29**
    *   **Problem:** The narration sounds synthetic and lacks natural conversational warmth and pacing.
    *   **Why it matters:** While clear, a robotic voice can feel impersonal and less encouraging to older learners who are already anxious about technology.
    *   **Recommended fix:** Re-record narration with a human voice actor using a warmer, more conversational tone.

## Tips for the instructor using this video on Monday
1.  **Be ready on the pause button:** You *must* manually pause the video at 2:02, 3:00, and 6:44 to allow learners time to complete the requested activities.
2.  **Provide a code handout:** Because the code at 3:44 is small, print out the starter HTML code on paper so learners with low vision can read it comfortably at their desks.
3.  **Demonstrate the testing live:** The testing sequence in Chapter 7 (4:26) is very fast. After playing that chapter, pause and demonstrate typing "library" and "zzz" live on your own projected screen, narrating your actions slowly.
4.  **Reiterate the `.txt` warning:** When learners save their files (Chapter 6), walk around the lab to ensure Windows Notepad hasn't secretly saved their files as `resource-finder.html.txt`.
5.  **Emphasize the security warnings:** Pause after 3:33 and 7:17 to have a brief class discussion about why we never put real passwords into AI prompts or trust fake login screens.
6.  **Manage expectations:** Remind them of the quote at 0:21—they are learning to direct AI and test prototypes, not trying to become professional software engineers today.
