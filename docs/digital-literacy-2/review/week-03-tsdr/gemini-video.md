# Week 3 video evaluation

## Verdict
This video is highly effective in its visual design and plain-language explanations, successfully reducing cognitive load for older novices through simplified interfaces. However, it is only usable with instructor support because the narrator does not leave any time for learners to respond to interactive prompts; the instructor must actively manage the playback. 

Overall score: 4/5.

## Evidence by dimension

### 1. Accuracy and correctness
*   **0:42:** The advice to "use a real heading style" rather than just bolding text is accurate and a crucial digital literacy standard.
*   **2:47:** The formula `=SUM(B2:B4)` is the correct, standard syntax for modern spreadsheet applications like Excel or Google Sheets.
*   **4:34:** The technical distinction between cropping (removing outer parts) and resizing (changing dimensions) is entirely accurate.
*   **5:50:** Accurately describes a CSV as carrying plain table data without preserving formatting or formulas.

### 2. Clarity of explanation
*   **0:50:** Clearly explains *why* bold text isn't a heading by referencing assistive technology and navigation panes.
*   **2:08:** Breaks down the spreadsheet grid simply for novices: columns have letters, rows have numbers, and cell addresses combine them.
*   **2:54:** Explains the colon in a formula range in plain English ("include the cells from the first address through the last").

### 3. Pacing and cognitive load for older adults
*   **0:40:** The typing animation for the heading is slightly fast, but acceptable because the screen is uncluttered.
*   **2:43:** The step-by-step entry of the formula allows enough time to process the action visually.
*   **3:22:** The prompt to "pause and predict" is immediately followed by the next instruction, giving the learner zero time to react, which spikes cognitive load.

### 4. Practice prompts
*   **1:54:** The narrator asks the learner to pause and propose an improvement, but the audio continues instantly.
*   **3:22:** The narrator asks the learner to predict the formula result, but reveals the answer immediately.
*   **6:23:** The narrator asks the learner to test the pack with a partner, but moves straight into the next checklist item.

### 5. Audio–visual alignment
*   **0:42:** The mouse clicks "Heading 1" exactly as the narrator says "Use a real heading style."
*   **3:03:** The visual changes the value from 12 to 15 exactly as the narration describes it.
*   **3:32:** The chart data selection highlights exactly when the narrator mentions selecting labels and values.

### 6. Screen demonstrations
*   **0:42:** A clear red circle appears around the cursor to indicate a click, which is excellent for novices following the pointer.
*   **2:47:** The formula is typed out clearly in a large, simplified cell, making the exact keystrokes obvious.
*   **5:00:** The video editing interface is abstracted to clear, large buttons (Start, Explain, Pause, Result) making the concept easy to grasp without software clutter.

### 7. Visual design and legibility
*   **2:04:** The spreadsheet uses a massive font and high contrast (dark green/white), ideal for low vision and classroom projectors.
*   **4:14:** Slide design principles are shown with large, unambiguous icons and minimal text.
*   **5:39:** File formats are represented by large, distinct icons with clear labels.

### 8. Narration quality
*   **2:13:** The pronunciation of "B2" and other cell references is clear and distinct.
*   **3:18:** The series of questions ("Did you type... Did the range omit...") sounds robotic and lacks natural conversational intonation.
*   **Overall:** The voice is clearly a Text-to-Speech engine. While intelligible, it lacks the human warmth and encouragement that builds confidence in older adult learners.

### 9. Relevance and motivation
*   **0:16:** Frames the exercise around a "community computer help session," a realistic and motivating volunteer task for veterans.
*   **6:08:** Addresses the real-world issue of image permissions and licenses, which builds responsible digital citizenship.
*   **7:00:** Explicitly connects the skills to everyday tasks like a "household budget" or "community notice."

### 10. Accessibility
*   **1:10:** Explicitly teaches not to rely on color or position alone for links, modeling good accessibility practices.
*   **2:25:** Explains the meaning of the numbers (dollars vs. boxes) so a visually impaired user understands the context of the data.
*   **4:50:** Reminds the user to provide alternative text for images.

*   **2:04:** The simplified, high-contrast spreadsheet is brilliant for reducing extraneous cognitive load.
*   **2:54:** Translating the formula syntax (the colon) into plain English.
*   **4:34:** The clear visual and verbal distinction between cropping and resizing.

## Tips for the instructor using this video on Monday
*   **Keep your hand on the pause button:** You must manually pause the video at 1:54, 3:22, and 6:23, as the video will not wait for the learners to complete the prompts.
*   **Have learners write down their prediction:** At 3:22, pause the video and have learners physically write down their predicted formula total before you hit play to reveal the answer.
*   **Demonstrate real software:** The video uses highly simplified, fictional interfaces to teach concepts. Show a real Word/Docs ribbon to map the "Heading 1" dropdown (0:42) to their actual software.
*   **Show a real video editor:** After Chapter 8 (5:00), briefly open a real tool (like Windows Clipchamp) to show what the trim and split tools actually look like.
*   **Reiterate the accessibility rules:** Emphasize the rules mentioned in the video (using real headings, descriptive link text, and alt text) as they are crucial for the week's objective of "creating for a person."
*   **Pair them up:** Dedicate the last 15 minutes of class to Chapter 10 (6:23), pairing learners up to test each other's resource packs as suggested.
