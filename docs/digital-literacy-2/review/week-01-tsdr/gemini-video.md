## Evidence by dimension

### 1. Accuracy and correctness
- **0:56**: The keyboard shortcuts mentioned (`Ctrl` and `+` to zoom in, `Ctrl` and `0` to reset) are standard and correct for Chrome, Edge, and Firefox.
- **2:50**: The distinction between processing, working memory, and saved storage is technically accurate and presented at an appropriate level for beginners.
- **3:41**: Advising users to check the printer destination first is highly accurate, as "Print to PDF" is a common source of confusion on modern PCs.
- **5:04**: The explanation of "Free/Busy" calendar sharing accurately reflects how modern calendar systems (like Outlook or Google Calendar) handle basic privacy.

### 2. Clarity of explanation
- **1:34**: The distinction between "Page zoom" (browser only) and "Display scaling" (whole system) is explained clearly using a side-by-side visual comparison.
- **3:00**: The analogy comparing working memory to a "workbench" and storage to a "filing cabinet" is brilliant for older adults, making an abstract concept concrete.
- **4:41**: The explanation of why to use different calendar views (Day for details, Week for conflicts, Month for overview) gives clear, logical reasons for interface features.
- **5:11**: The visual comparison showing exactly what "Your calendar" shows versus what the "Partner sees" instantly clarifies the concept of shared availability.

### 3. Pacing and cognitive load for older adults
- **0:51**: The simulated mouse clicks to open the browser menu and select zoom happen quite quickly, which might be hard for a novice to track.
- **2:47**: The conceptual diagrams build up slowly, one element at a time, which keeps cognitive load low.
- **4:26**: The simulated typing of the calendar event details ("Library practice", times, location) appears almost instantly, which is faster than a real user would experience.
- **Overall**: The speech rate is deliberate and steady, giving learners time to process the spoken information.

- **5:30**: The narrator asks learners to "Pause and decide," but gives the answer 6 seconds later.
- **6:25**: The prompt "Now pause and choose one improvement..." is immediately followed by more instructions, offering zero silence to actually begin the task.

## Top issues, ranked

1. **Severity: High** | **Timestamps: 1:18, 1:59, 5:30, 6:25**
   - **The problem:** The narrator asks learners to pause or answer a question, but the video continues almost immediately without a visual cue or sufficient silence.
   - **Why it matters:** Older adult learners with low tech confidence need time to process instructions, move their hands, and attempt the task. Rushing them causes frustration and breaks the learning cycle.
   - **Recommended fix:** re-edit visuals (insert a 5-to-10 second title card with a clear "Pause the video now" icon at these moments).

2. **Severity: Medium** | **Timestamps: 0:00 to 7:07**
   - **The problem:** The narration is synthetic/AI-generated, resulting in a monotone delivery lacking natural inflection.
   - **Why it matters:** This specific audience benefits greatly from human connection, warmth, and an encouraging tone to overcome technology anxiety. A robotic voice can feel alienating.
   - **Recommended fix:** re-record narration.

3. **Severity: Low** | **Timestamps: 0:51, 4:26**
   - **The problem:** The simulated mouse pointer is standard size, and simulated typing appears instantly.
   - **Why it matters:** Learners with reduced vision may lose track of the small pointer, and novices may not realize what is happening if text appears too quickly.
   - **Recommended fix:** re-edit visuals (enlarge the mouse pointer and slow down the typing animations).

## Tips for the instructor using this video on Monday
- Keep your hand on the pause button. You must manually pause the video at 1:18, 1:59, 5:30, and after the instructions at 6:43 to give learners actual time to practice.
- Warn the class beforehand that the narrator is a computer-generated voice, so they aren't put off by the robotic tone.
- At 0:51, pause and demonstrate the zoom function live on the specific browsers installed in your lab, as the menu locations might differ slightly from the simulation.
- Provide scrap paper and pens at every workstation before the lesson begins, referencing the tip at 0:29.
- Reiterate the "don't keep hitting print" rule (4:04) strongly, as this is a common issue in shared computer labs.
- During the final independent check (6:24), walk around the room to facilitate the "tell a partner" step, as some learners may be hesitant to initiate this themselves.
