# Review framework: Tell, Show, Do, Review inside WIPPEA + adult learning theory

## Course goal (from the instructor)
Use **Tell, Show, Do, Review** to help adult learners understand the topics in IC3 Digital Literacy GS6 Level 2 (weeks 1–5), plus a bonus week 6 that teaches the basics of agentic engineering a SaaS website.

## Primary teaching method: Tell, Show, Do, Review
Every skill or objective should get its own short cycle. Avoid one long "tell" block followed by one long "do" block.
| Step | What it means | Evidence to look for | WIPPEA link |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Tell** | Explain what the skill is, why it matters to the learner, the key words, and the steps, in plain language | A slide or narration that states the purpose and the steps; vocabulary defined at first use | Introduction + Presentation (explanation) |
| **Show** | Demonstrate the exact task step by step while learners watch; point out what to look for and common mistakes | Instructor demo on the projector, a video chapter or a screen demo of the same task learners will do | Presentation (modeling) |
| **Do** | Learners perform the task themselves, first guided, then on their own, with feedback | A worksheet task or lab step that repeats what was shown, on their own workstation, with enough time | Practice |
| **Review** | Check each learner can do it, correct mistakes, recap the key points, and connect to real use | A performance check or knowledge check tied to the objective, a recap, and a transfer or next-use prompt | Evaluation + Application (and the next week's Warm-up) |

Questions to ask for each skill: Is there a Tell? Is the Show the same task as the Do? Does the Do happen soon after the Show, with enough time? Is there a Review that checks each learner, not just the class?

## WIPPEA lesson cycle
Source: U.S. Department of Education, TEAL Center Fact Sheet No. 8, *Effective Lesson Planning* (adapted from Hunter, 1982, *Mastery Teaching*). A cyclical model: learners should show mastery at each step before the instructor moves on. The cycle repeats for each objective, and the evaluation activity is designed first (backward design, Wiggins & McTighe).

| Stage | Purpose | What good looks like |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **W — Warm-up** | Activate and check prior knowledge; review earlier material relevant to today | A short activity reviewing previously learned content (here: Basic Computer Skills, Level 1), plus one that points toward today's topic |
| **I — Introduction** | Give a broad overview and focus attention on the new lesson | State (and show in writing) the objectives; explain the content and its benefits by relating it to learners' own lives; ask questions to surface what learners already know about the new material |
| **P — Presentation** | Teach the new content and concepts | Introduce new vocabulary; use several modalities (visuals, real objects, description, explanation, text); check understanding during the presentation and adjust |
| **P — Practice** | Model the skill, then guided practice | Instructor models first; varied group, pair and individual activities practising what was presented; technology used where available; moves from guided toward independent |
| **E — Evaluation** | Assess each learner's attainment of the objective | Oral, written or performance checks tied directly to the stated objectives; scaffolds for lower levels (e.g., a word bank); tells the instructor whether to reteach |
| **A — Application** | Transfer to new situations beyond the lesson and to learners' own lives | Activities learners care about; real-life use (e.g., at home); follow-up in later classes to see what further support is needed |

Also from the fact sheet:
- **Objectives** must be specific, outcome-based, measurable and describe learner behaviour. Use **ABCD**: Audience, Behavior (an observable verb), Condition (circumstances), Degree (how well).
- **Backward design**: decide what evidence shows mastery, then plan activities that prepare learners for that evaluation.
- **Differentiation** is planned from learner profiles.
- **Ongoing checks** let the instructor change course mid-lesson.

## Adult learning theory (Knowles' andragogy)
1. **Need to know**: adults want to know why they are learning something before they invest in it.
2. **Self-concept**: adults are self-directing; they respond to choice, respect and control over pace.
3. **Experience**: adults' prior experience is a resource (and sometimes a source of habits to unlearn); instruction should draw on it.
4. **Readiness**: adults learn what helps them handle real situations in their lives now.
5. **Orientation**: learning is problem-centred and task-centred, not subject-centred.
6. **Motivation**: internal motivators (confidence, independence, quality of life) outweigh external ones.

Practical checks for this audience (older veterans, many retired, finished Basic Computer Skills and Level 1):
- Relevance is explicit and concrete (appointments, reading comfortably, printing, getting help).
- Pace allows for reduced vision, hearing and dexterity; time to read, try and ask.
- Low-risk practice: fictional data, can undo, no embarrassment for mistakes.
- Respectful tone: not childish; builds on competence already gained.
- Chunking: one skill at a time, with frequent checks.
- Transfer: learners leave able to do the task on their own device.

## Content correctness
- Every instruction, term and behaviour described must match how current mainstream tools actually work (2026: Windows 11, Microsoft Edge, Google Chrome, Microsoft 365/Outlook or Google Calendar, common printers).
- Vendor-neutral wording is fine, but it must not be misleading; flag advice that would fail or confuse on a typical lab computer.
- Slides, lesson plan, worksheet, answer key and video must agree with each other (names, numbers, steps).
